The question

During qualifying, the broadcast splits every lap into three sectors and colors each one purple (fastest of the session), green (personal best) or yellow (slower than personal best). The colors tell you that a driver gained or lost time, but each sector is 25 to 30 seconds long and holds several corners, so they cannot say where or why.

Research question: In Formula 1 qualifying, which corners separate the fastest teams from the rest, and does each team have a type of corner (slow, medium or fast) where it gains the most time?

The audience is F1 fans who want to understand more than a results table shows: how racing lines, driving styles and car strengths turn into lap time.

For each of the 24 races of 2025, the project uses every driver's fastest qualifying lap. Qualifying laps are the fairest comparison: low fuel, new tires, no traffic and maximum effort.

Method

  1. Resample every lap by distance. Telemetry arrives a few times per second, so two drivers' samples land at different points on the track. Each lap is interpolated onto a common grid with a point every 5 m, so drivers can be compared at exactly the same spot.
  2. Cut a zone around each corner. From 150 m before the corner marker to 100 m after it, never overlapping the neighboring corner.
  3. Measure each corner. On the field-median lap (the middle speed of all 20 cars at every point): minimum (apex) speed, exit speed and braking distance.
  4. Group the corners with K-means. All 403 corners of the season are clustered into slow, medium and fast types. K-means is implemented by hand in NumPy (with k-means++ starts and 20 restarts). PCA reduces the three features to two axes for the scatter plot.
  5. Measure the teams. For each team's faster driver, the time through every corner zone is compared with the median team. Averaging those gaps per corner type gives each team's fingerprint.

Robustness check: re-running the pipeline with a 2.5 m or 10 m grid instead of 5 m kept 97% of corners in the same type, moved team fingerprints by at most 3 ms and left the team ranking unchanged.

Findings

    Example (Australia, Norris vs Verstappen): both drivers brake at almost the same points, but Norris carries more speed through the apex in 10 of 14 corners and is back on full throttle 10 to 25 m earlier. Verstappen gains in the first corner of each sequence, where his higher straight-line speed counts; Norris gains more back in the corners that follow. Select Australia on the main page and click T3 or T4 on the map to see it in the speed, throttle and brake traces.

    Reading the charts

    Track map
    One dot per corner, in the color of whichever selected team was faster there. Bigger dot = bigger gap. Click a corner to zoom the speed trace to it.
    Season fingerprint
    Average milliseconds gained (teal, negative) or lost (red, positive) per corner against the median team, over the whole season.
    Speed, throttle and brake
    Both drivers over one lap, aligned by distance. Shaded bands are corner zones. Ctrl + scroll to zoom.
    Corner grouping (PCA)
    Every corner of the season placed by its speed (left to right) and braking (top to bottom), colored by type. Fast corners line up because almost all are taken flat out.
    Why three corner types?
    The silhouette score for each number of clusters. Higher means better separated groups.

    Limitations

    • One lap per team per race. Results mix car and driver: a team's faster driver represents the car.
    • Sampling. Telemetry comes a few times per second, so differences under about 10 m (or a few km/h at a single point) are within the noise.
    • Corner zones are fixed windows. Gains on straights are not counted, and in tight sequences the first corner's zone includes the end of the straight before it.
    • Unofficial data. Timing and corner positions come from unofficial sources and can have gaps, as with the Miami lap above.

    What the data can be used for

    • Seeing how a pole lap was won, corner by corner, instead of only the final gap.
    • Comparing car characteristics: which teams are strong in slow, traction-limited corners and which in fast, aerodynamic ones.
    • Predicting which tracks should suit which team, based on each track's mix of corner types.
    • Comparing seasons: the same pipeline can run on 2026 to see how the new regulations changed team strengths.
